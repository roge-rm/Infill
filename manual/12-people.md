# People
> Households, ages, schooling, wealth, work, health and getting about.

Every home is a household of children, adults and elderly, and the town keeps count of each one.

## Households

A household fills its home until the 1950s. After that families get smaller, and by 2010 a home holds about seven in eight of the people it would have, so a town needs more homes for the same people. Seven in ten adults work. Children grow up in about sixteen years, adults grow old, and the old die, sooner in poor health.

Births fall in the depression and the war, boom from 1947 to the early 1960s, then fall again. Fewer children die as the century goes on, and the old live longer, most of all from the 1950s. Newcomers are better schooled as the years go by, and come as young families in the boom and older ones since.

When a home is too full, young adults move out first. A home with no adults left goes empty and up for sale, and sells sooner when the town is short of homes.

## Wealth

Households are poor, middling or well off. Who moves into a new home depends on its land value and on the jobs the town has filled: schooled jobs bring middling households and graduate jobs well-off ones. Better-off households pay more tax, spend more in the shops, drive more, and are healthier. They also mind pollution and crime more.

A household's wealth follows its grown-ups' schooling over the years: one where most went to high school rises a step now and then, and one where most had no schooling falls.

## Priced out and sleeping rough

When land gets dear, poor households are priced out now and then, and a better-off household takes the home. **Rent control** in a district, or the **Social housing** law (1945), keeps them in. While the town is short of homes, some of those priced out, or cleared out by bulldozing, end up sleeping rough. Many move on within months. A **Shelter** takes in 40 of them. The People window counts them, the parks show their bedrolls, and they weigh on what people think of housing.

## Schooling

Grown-ups are schooled to none, school or high school. What children get depends on the schools within reach and how crowded they are, and it builds up over years. Jobs want workers with the right schooling: workshops and farms take almost anyone, banks and offices want people who went to school and high school. A worker can take a job at their own level or below.

When a kind of business can't find enough of the workers it needs, it stops growing, and the advice line says so.

## Work

Workers take the nearest jobs with room, best schooled first. Anyone who can't reach a job within an hour and a half is out of work, and so is anyone left over. People out of work push up theft. In a region, people can work in the next town over.

From 2000, some well-schooled people with broadband work from home.

## Getting about

Each trip to work or the shops goes the quickest way: on foot, by car, bus, trolleybus, tram, subway or train. How many households have a car goes up from almost none before 1910 to most by 1980, fewer for the poor and more for the well off. A long commute puts people off a home.

## Health

Health comes from doctors, mains water and the sewer, wealth, parks, clean air and ambulances, and suffers from pollution, grime, smog, crowding, dumps and brownfield. After a flood, homes on wells and septic tanks can get sick. Now and then an epidemic goes round, worse in crowded tenements and apartments and better with care, mains water and sewers:

- **Cholera** comes from foul water. Homes on mains water and the sewer are much safer, and it's rare once the water's chlorinated, from 1915.
- **Consumption** spreads in crowded homes. A sanatorium holds it back, and it's rare once it can be cured, from 1947.
- **Influenza** goes round anywhere, worse in polluted air. The flu of 1918 always comes.

From 1945 antibiotics halve the deaths, and from 1955 vaccines save most children and make outbreaks rarer. Heat waves are hard on the elderly in hot homes.

## Moving in and out

People come when there are jobs to fill and homes to live in, and the town draws newcomers by itself as it grows. Nobody moves in without a way in: a road to the edge of the map, a station on a line out, a port or an airport. On an island with sea all round, they come by boat. A railway station on a line out and an airport bring more. People leave when their homes shrink, burn or flood, or when they're bulldozed with nowhere else to go, and news of that puts newcomers off for a while.
