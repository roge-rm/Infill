# Roads and traffic
> Roads, crossings, bridges and tunnels, and how the town drives on them.

Everything in town needs a road. Lots grow up to three tiles from one, and a road reaching the edge of the map is what ties the town to the outside.

When a new road reaches the edge, the game asks whether to link it to the outside or keep it in town. A linked road lets newcomers, trade and commuters in and out; one kept in town is just a road along the edge. Inspect a road on the edge to change it later.

## Laying roads

Pick **Road** under **Transport**, choose a kind in the tray and drag. The road goes along one way and then the other in an L, and the way you first move decides which comes first. Drawing over an old road upgrades it, and drawing over a worn one of the same kind relays it for less than new.

**With pipes under it** lays a water main, a sewer and a storm drain under the road as it goes, using the best pipes of the day. It's the easy way to get mains to a new street.

A road can't go through buildings, it crosses track only straight over as a level crossing, and it crosses water only on a bridge.

## Kinds of road

Each kind has a price a tile, upkeep, a speed and how much traffic it takes before it slows.

- **Dirt road** and **Gravel road** are cheap and slow, for the early town and the edges.
- **Lane** is the cheapest and slowest, and can't take a bridge.
- **Street** is the everyday road, and **One-way street** carries a bit more, in the way you drag it.
- **Avenue** and **One-way avenue** come in 1910 and carry twice what a street does.
- **Boulevard** comes in 1920. It's two carriageways with a median, drawn straight. You draw the right-hand side and the other is added beside it, running the other way. Traffic crosses the median only where a road crosses it and at the ends.
- **Highway** comes in 1955 in the Motor age. It's fast and carries five times a street, but nothing is built along it, no one walks on it, it lowers land value beside it, and other roads join it only at an interchange.

## Crossings

A road tile with roads on three or four sides is a crossing, and every crossing makes traffic wait. Pick **Traffic** and drag over crossings to set what controls them.

- With no control, it handles a little traffic with almost no wait.
- **Stop signs** handle a bit more, with a longer wait.
- **Roundabout** (from 1905) handles a good deal, with a short wait.
- **Traffic lights** (from 1920) handle the most of the ordinary kinds, with the longest wait when it's quiet.
- **Interchange** (from 1950) is expensive and handles anything, with no wait. Drawing a highway across a road, or a road across a highway, builds one for you with ramps on the corners.
- **Town's choice** lets the town put up stop signs and lights as traffic grows.

The more a crossing is asked to take beyond what its control handles, the longer the wait, up to four minutes. Inspect a crossing to see how long it takes to get through.

## Traffic

Every month the town works out its trips: people to work and to the shops, freight from the works to the edge, and goods to whoever buys them. Each trip takes the quickest way it can, on foot, by car or by transit, and trips go out across the month a part at a time, so later ones see the roads the earlier ones filled.

A road at its capacity takes half as long again to cross, and up to three times as long when it's well over. Level crossings and opening bridges hold traffic up too. Busy roads are noisy, which lowers land value, and slow traffic puts out more fumes.

**Traffic flowing** in the Traffic view is how long trips would take on clear roads, as a share of how long they take. Shops and offices do better when traffic flows, and the Renewal era asks for 55% or better.

How many people can drive goes up with the years: almost no one before 1910, a fifth of households by 1925, more than half by 1955 and most by 1980. Poorer households have fewer cars and better-off ones more.

## Bridges

A road or track across water is a bridge, straight and one span to a stretch of water. **Cheapest bridge** picks the cheapest kind that spans it in the year, or you can pick one:

- **Trestle bridge**: cheap, short and low. Ships can't pass and trucks can't use it.
- **Swing bridge** and **Lift bridge** (from 1920): open for ships, holding up the traffic while they do.
- **Truss bridge**: high enough for ships, up to eight tiles.
- **Concrete bridge** (from 1930): low, cheap to keep, and long lived.
- **Suspension bridge** (from 1930) and **Cable-stayed bridge** (from 1975): long and high, for roads only.

A low bridge that would cut a port off from the sea isn't allowed. Suspension and cable-stayed bridges, and truss bridges of six tiles or more, close in a gale and stay closed for two days after.

Bridges wear. Heavy trucks wear them faster, a worn one is posted for no trucks, and one worn right out is closed as unsafe until it's relaid.

Inspect a bridge to **Add toll** or **Close bridge**. The toll is set in the Budget, in steps of five cents. Drivers would rather go round a toll, and trucks even more so, and what they pay comes in as **Dues and tolls**.

## Tunnels

Turn on **As a tunnel** to take a road (from 1920) or track under hills, water or the town. A tunnel is straight, at least three tiles, with an opening on dry land at each end. It costs more under water, and more again where it has to be dug out from under buildings and roads.

Tunnels need power for their pumps, on or beside them. Without it they flood in a downpour and close until they're mended. Trucks carrying fuel oil aren't allowed in road tunnels.

## Parking

There are no car parks to build. The **Limit parking** district policy has fewer people drive.
