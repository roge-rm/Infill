# Zones and growth
> What each zone grows, what a lot needs to grow, demand and land value.

You don't build homes, shops or works yourself. You zone land for them and the town builds what it wants there, when it wants it.

## Zones

- **Residential**: homes.
- **Commercial**: shops, then hotels, banks and department stores.
- **Industrial**: workshops, mills, warehouses, factories and works.
- **Offices**: offices, then office towers and skyscrapers. They come with the Streetcar era; before that, office work is done in the shops and banks.
- **Mixed use** (from the Streetcar era): flats over shops, for main streets.
- **Farmland**: farms, orchards and market gardens, woodlots and, on a seam, mines and oil wells.

Zoning costs $5 a tile, or $2 for rural.

## Densities

Each zone is painted with a density, which is the tallest it can grow:

- **Rural**: big lots in the country, for homes and shops only. Rural lots grow only farmsteads, cabins, country houses and the like, and need almost nothing.
- **Low density**: cottages to large houses, stores and shops, workshops and mills.
- **Medium density**: row houses and tenements, main street blocks, warehouses and factories.
- **High density**: apartments, department stores, works and office towers.
- **Towers** (from the Motor age): tower blocks, skyscrapers and the tallest buildings. Not for industry.

Changing the density of land that's already zoned is free.

## Sizes

Every zone has buildings on one lot, on two, and on four, from cottages and villas to mansions, and shops to covered markets. A two-lot building runs along the road or back from it. Where there's room, the town more often puts up a bigger building, and the smaller ones fill the gaps between. Up to medium density a bigger building can take in the lots of lesser buildings beside it, but never ones as good as itself.

## How a lot grows

Each kind of building has a ladder, and a lot climbs it a rung at a time, from a cottage to a house to a large house to row houses and up. Each rung needs:

- a road within three tiles (a highway doesn't count),
- a density that allows it, and its year to have come,
- enough appeal, and for homes, shops and offices enough land value,
- the power, mains water and sewer it needs,
- and for shops, offices and works, enough workers with the schooling they want.

The first rung of every ladder needs nothing but the road. Most second rungs want power, the next mains water, and medium density and up the sewer as well. Towers also need a ladder company within reach for fires.

New buildings take time to go up, longer for bigger ones, and twice as long in freezing weather. A building has to stand at least half a year before it's pulled down for something bigger, and its people move out while the new one goes up. Big buildings take in the lots beside them.

A building that loses the power, water or sewer it needs may drop a rung. So may one in a deep flood.

## Appeal

How much a lot appeals to builders depends on its zone.

- Homes like land value, low crime, clean air, quiet, a short commute, mains water, the sewer, power, a phone and later broadband. What they expect grows with the years: a home without mains water was normal in 1900 and is a poor one by 1945. They don't like industry next door, airport noise, smog, summer heat, uncollected garbage or old age.
- Shops like people nearby, passing trade, traffic that flows and stock from town.
- Offices like dear land, shops nearby, a college within 30 tiles and good phones.
- Works mostly want their freight to get out, and like water nearby.

Crime and rackets put everyone off.

## Demand

The demand bars in the strip show what the town wants, worked out again each week. Tap them for the Demand window: for each zone, how much is wanted or how much too much, the sum it comes from, the zone's tax, and what's stopping it growing. Under them, how many are out of work, the jobs nobody's doing, the homes for sale and whether newcomers have a way in.

- Homes are wanted when there are more jobs than people to fill them, plus newcomers. Nobody moves in without a way in. A railway station on a line to the edge brings more, and so does an airport. People forced out of town put newcomers off for a while.
- Shops are wanted for what the town's people spend. Better-off people spend more.
- Works are wanted for the outside market, which grows with the town and the years and rises and falls with the economy. A road to the edge, a freight yard, a freight terminal and a port all make it bigger. Works are also wanted for goods the town brings in that it could make.
- Offices, once they come in, are wanted more and more as the town and the years grow.
- Farms are wanted for food and timber, and for the market.
- Mixed use takes from both homes and shops when both are wanted.

Lower taxes make every zone more wanted and higher taxes less. When there's more than the town wants, the least appealing buildings step down a rung, and empty homes go first.

## Land value

Land value goes up near water, trees, parks, shops and offices, busy neighbourhoods, transit stops and stations, and with police and fire cover. It goes down with pollution, crime, noise, foul water, industry next door, a dump, power pylons, a highway, a jail, brownfield, busy roads and floods. It also decides who moves into new homes: poor or middling on cheap land, middling or well off on dear land.

## Rezoning and bulldozing

Zoning over a building with a different zone leaves it standing, marked as not fitting the zoning. An empty home comes down soon, and anything else once it's at least 30 years old. Rural land zoned for more becomes the same rung of the ordinary ladder.

Bulldozing costs $15 a tile, plus what the owners are owed: more for homes and businesses on dear land, a quarter for empty homes, and double for heritage buildings. The people living there move to empty homes, and anyone left over leaves town. The jobs are lost, and the neighbours are upset for a while, which puts homes nearby off.

From 1960, works and mines that close leave brownfield behind, and nothing grows there until it's cleaned up. Bulldoze it to clean it up.

## Farmland

Farmland grows what's under it: a mine on iron ore or coal, an oil well on oil, a woodlot in trees, and a farm on open land. A farm off good soil grows half as much. The **Land** view shows what's in the ground.

Mines and wells work their seams out: five years for each tile of seam under and around them. Then they close, their jobs go, and they leave brownfield and no seam behind. Bulldoze brownfield to clean it up.
